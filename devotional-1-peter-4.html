1 and 2 Peter: Living Hope · Week 4 Devotional

Living for the Will of God

The fire isn't strange. It's a furnace.

1 Peter 4:1 to 19

Peter opens this chapter with a soldier's word, and he spends it on your mind. Arm yourselves. That isn't devotional language; that's a man telling you a war is on, and most of us have been walking through it unarmed.

Here's what makes the week land. Peter doesn't arm you for a battle you have to win. He arms you with a mind that's already been proved, the mind Christ carried into His own suffering, a settled decision to do the Father's will no matter what it cost. Once you've decided you'd rather suffer than sin, sin runs out of leverage over you. It can't buy you, and it can't scare you.

From there Peter draws a line down the middle of your life. The time that's past was enough for the old passions; the rest of it belongs to the will of God. Then he tells you what to spend it on. Love that strains. A door that opens. A gift used on somebody else. When the fire comes, and Peter says when rather than if, you hand your soul to a faithful Creator and you keep doing good. Five days in the furnace, with the One who's already been through it.

01

Arm Yourself With His Mind

Since therefore Christ suffered in the flesh, arm yourselves with the same way of thinking, for whoever has suffered in the flesh has ceased from sin, so as to live for the rest of the time in the flesh no longer for human passions but for the will of God.

1 Peter 4:1 to 2 · ESV

Notice what Peter hands you, and notice where he tells you to put it. He hands you a weapon; he straps it to your mind. The Christian life doesn't get defended by good intentions. It gets armed.

Watch what he says the weapon is. Not a technique, not a rule, not a promise you make about Tuesday. The same way of thinking Christ had. Peter knows exactly where that mind was forged, because he was standing nearby when it happened. In a garden, on the worst night of His life, Jesus said not my will, but yours, be done. That wasn't a mood that came over Him. It was a decision made in the dark, before the soldiers ever showed up.

Peter has a painful reason for making this point. That same night, he had no such resolve, and he denied his Lord before morning. He isn't scolding you from above; he's telling you what he learned the hard way about deciding before the moment comes.

Then read the promise carefully, because it's better than it first sounds. Whoever has suffered in the flesh has ceased from sin. Peter isn't saying you'll never fail again. He's saying sin is no longer the boss. Sin rules by promising you comfort and threatening you with pain; once you've decided you'll suffer rather than disobey, it has nothing left to bargain with.

The Greek is hoplizō (ὁπλίζω): to arm oneself, to take up weapons, to put on armor. It's a soldier's word, and here's what should stop you: this is the only place in the whole New Testament it appears, and Peter spends it on the mind. Not on your circumstances, not on your enemies, not on your strength. Your thinking. Peter is telling you the battle over how you live gets won or lost between your ears, so arm it. If you've been overrun this year, the honest question isn't whether the enemy was strong. It's whether you walked out of the house dressed for a fight.

Decide before the temptation comes that you'd rather suffer than sin, and sin runs out of leverage.

Reflect

What decision do you need to make today, in the calm, that you'll never make well in the moment?

Where has sin been buying you with comfort, or scaring you with pain?

Jesus settled the Father's will in a garden before anyone came for Him. What's your garden this week?

02

The Line Down the Middle of Your Life

For the time that is past suffices for doing what the Gentiles want to do, living in sensuality, passions, drunkenness, orgies, drinking parties, and lawless idolatry. With respect to this they are surprised when you do not join them in the same flood of debauchery, and they malign you.

1 Peter 4:3 to 4 · ESV

Peter draws a line down the middle of your life, and he's generous about which side is behind you. The time that's past suffices. Enough. You've given the old passions plenty of your years; you don't owe them any more.

There's mercy hiding in that word. Peter doesn't ask you to go back and re-litigate every one of those years. He asks you to stop adding to them. There comes a point where you quit rehearsing the old life and spend what's left on purpose, and Peter says let that point be today.

Now look at what it cost them, because verse 4 was not a small thing in their world. Work ran through the trade guilds, and the guilds feasted in the temples. Join the dyers or the stonemasons and you ate meat from the god's altar and poured the first cup to the god and to Caesar. Peter's list wasn't some hidden underworld; it was the social calendar. It was how business got done.

When a believer quit those feasts, the whole street knew. One empty seat insulted your host, your guild, and the gods of your city. Contracts dried up. Invitations stopped. Neighbors called you disloyal. Nobody pours a cup to Caesar at the office party anymore, yet you know exactly what it costs to be the one who won't run with the flood.

The Greek is xenizō (ξενίζω): to strike as strange, to astonish; also, to receive as a guest, to host a stranger. One word doing two jobs, and Peter uses both. Here in verse 4 your old crowd is xenizo at you; they look at your new life and treat you like a foreigner in your own town. Hold onto the word, because Peter isn't finished with it. He'll turn it around in verse 12 and aim it at the fire, and in between the two he'll drop the other half of the family, the word for loving a stranger. The world makes strangers out of people. The church makes room for them.

You've given the passions enough of your years. Give the rest to God.

Reflect

What would change if you truly believed the past is enough and owes you nothing more?

Where does not running with the flood cost you something real right now?

Have you been mistaking being treated as strange for evidence you've done something wrong?

03

Love That Strains, Doors That Open

Above all, keep loving one another earnestly, since love covers a multitude of sins. Show hospitality to one another without grumbling.

1 Peter 4:8 to 9 · ESV

Peter has just told you the clock is running, and this is what he wants you doing with the time. Not something heroic. Love the people in front of you, and open your door.

That word earnestly is ektenes, stretched out, strained, the way a runner's muscles strain at full effort. Peter isn't describing a warm feeling; he's describing love that costs you something and keeps going anyway. Then he tells you why it goes above all the rest. Love covers a multitude of sins. In a church under pressure, people get on each other's nerves and old offenses stack up in the corner. Earnest love keeps covering, keeps forgiving, keeps the family together when the world outside is against it.

Then he gets specific in a way that costs money and Saturday. Show hospitality without grumbling. In their world there were no church buildings. The church met around somebody's table, and a traveling believer either found a Christian's open door or took his chances in a roadside inn no decent person trusted. Your open home wasn't a nice extra; it was the church's infrastructure.

Notice he adds without grumbling, which tells you he knew exactly what he was asking. Hospitality was never about an impressive house. It's an open door and a warm welcome for somebody who needs one. Set one more place at the table this week. A table and a chair can preach.

The Greek is philoxenos (φιλόξενος): hospitable; literally a lover of strangers, from philos, love, and xenos, stranger. Look at the second half of that word, because you met it yesterday. Xenos is the root under xenizo, the strangeness Peter has been tracking through the whole chapter. The world treats you as a stranger for leaving the feast; God's answer isn't to make you bitter about strangers, it's to make you a lover of them. That's the whole chapter in three words. The world makes strangers. The church makes room. They treat you like a foreigner, and you answer by setting them a place at the table.

Hospitality was never about an impressive house. It's an open door and a warm welcome for somebody who needs one.

Reflect

Who is the person your love has stopped stretching toward?

What offense have you been storing instead of covering?

Who could you put in the extra chair this week, and what's really stopping you?

04

Grace Dyed to Match

As each has received a gift, use it to serve one another, as good stewards of God's varied grace: whoever speaks, as one who speaks oracles of God; whoever serves, as one who serves by the strength that God supplies, in order that in everything God may be glorified through Jesus Christ. To him belong glory and dominion forever and ever. Amen.

1 Peter 4:10 to 11 · ESV

Read the middle of that verse slowly if you're tired today. Whoever serves, let him serve by the strength that God supplies. You were never supposed to run the ministry on your own tank. God supplies the strength; you spend it.

Then Peter tells you why that arrangement matters so much, and it isn't only about your energy. If you served on your own power, you'd get the credit. Because the strength is His, the glory is His. That's exactly how it should be, and it's why burning yourself out isn't the holy option you've been told it is. It quietly puts your name where His belongs.

Now catch the word he sets in front of grace, because it reaches back to where this series started. God's varied grace. Many colored. Go back to chapter 1, verse 6, where Peter says you've been grieved by various trials. Same word in the Greek. Many colored trials in chapter 1; many colored grace in chapter 4.

Sit in that for a second. There's no shade the fire can come in that grace doesn't come dyed to match. Whatever color your particular trial is, there's a grace made for exactly that one. Then look at who carries it into the room. Stewards. Not warehouses; stewards, people handing out a color somebody else needs. The grace that matched your trial last year is in your hands now for somebody sitting three rows over.

The Greek is poikilos (ποικίλος): varied, diverse, many colored, variegated. The word describes something with many hues woven through it, like a patterned cloth. Peter uses it exactly twice in this letter, and the pairing is deliberate: poikilos trials in 1:6, poikilos grace in 4:10. He's telling a suffering church that God's supply is as varied as their suffering. Your trial isn't a color God ran out of. Grace comes in that shade too, and He usually delivers it through a person.

Whatever shade the fire comes in, grace comes in a matching color.

Reflect

Where have you been serving on your own strength, and quietly taking the credit for it?

What color of grace has God handed you that somebody near you needs this week?

If God supplies the strength, what would you stop white knuckling today?

05

Entrust Your Soul to a Faithful Creator

Beloved, do not be surprised at the fiery trial when it comes upon you to test you, as though something strange were happening to you. But rejoice insofar as you share Christ's sufferings, that you may also rejoice and be glad when his glory is revealed. . . . Therefore let those who suffer according to God's will entrust their souls to a faithful Creator while doing good.

1 Peter 4:12 to 13, 19 · ESV

Peter names it directly, and he doesn't say if. He says when. Fire is coming. Then he says the thing that takes half the weight off it: don't act like something strange is happening.

So much of our pain gets doubled by shock. Underneath the shock sits a lie, the one that says a real Christian shouldn't be going through this, that the fire is evidence God walked off. Peter tears that up. Fire isn't strange for a follower of a crucified Lord. It's normal, and it has a purpose.

Watch how he turns it. Rejoice insofar as you share Christ's sufferings. When you suffer for His name you aren't being punished; you're being joined to Jesus. You're walking where He walked, and the same Spirit of glory that rested on Him rests on you.

Then comes the landing, the verse to carry out of the week. Entrust your souls to a faithful Creator while doing good. Look at both halves of it. You entrust, you deposit your soul into safe hands, because the One holding it is a faithful Creator who doesn't drop what He makes. Meanwhile you keep doing good. You don't go bitter, and you don't go silent. That's how a believer walks through fire.

Listen to where Peter got that word entrust, because he watched it happen. Hanging on the cross, Jesus prayed Psalm 31 out loud: Father, into your hands I commit my spirit. He did it in the dark, with nails in Him, with no control left; the Father didn't drop Him. He raised Him. When you entrust your soul to a faithful Creator, you're doing the last thing your Savior did, and you already know how that ended.

The Greek is pyrōsis (πύρωσις): a burning, a fiery ordeal; the word used for the smelting furnace that refines metal. Peter didn't reach for a word meaning disaster. He reached for the word meaning refinery. Back in chapter 1 he'd already said your faith is more precious than gold that perishes though it's tested by fire; now he names the fire itself. A furnace isn't there to destroy the gold. It's there to prove it and to bring the impurity up where it can be lifted off. Don't read your fire as abandonment. Read it as a furnace. God isn't burning you up. He's proving you real.

Jesus did it in the dark, with nails in Him, and the Father didn't drop Him. He raised Him.

Reflect

What fire have you been treating as strange, or as punishment, that Peter would call a furnace?

What exactly are you still holding that you need to deposit into the hands of a faithful Creator?

Entrusting comes with while doing good. Where have you gone quiet or bitter while you waited?

A Prayer of Identity

Father, thank You that Christ suffered in the flesh and settled Your will in the dark, long before the soldiers came. Arm my mind with that same resolve, so sin loses its grip and has nothing left to bargain with. Thank You that the time I've already spent is enough, and that the rest of it belongs to You. Teach me to love earnestly when it costs me, to open my door without grumbling, and to serve by the strength You supply, so the glory lands on You and not on me. Thank You that Your grace comes in every color my trials come in, and that You hand it to me through Your people. When the fire comes, keep me from shock. Remind me it's a furnace and not abandonment, that You're proving me and not destroying me. Into Your hands I commit my spirit, the way my Savior did, because You've never dropped one. In Jesus' name, Amen.

Declaration

I love earnestly, I open my home, and I serve by the strength God supplies.
I'm not surprised by the fire. It's a furnace, and it proves me real.
I entrust my soul to a faithful Creator, and I keep doing good.
I walk with victory, not to it, in Jesus' name. Amen.

Now go, and spend the rest of your time for the will of God. May your mind be armed with the resolve of Christ, so sin loses its grip. May you love earnestly, open your home, and serve by the strength God supplies. May you meet the fire without shock, knowing it's a furnace and not abandonment, and may you entrust your soul to a faithful Creator while doing good.
