Malachi: The Coming Messenger · Week 1 Devotional

A Love Questioned

God gave His best. Don’t hand Him your leftovers.

Malachi 1:1 to 14

Malachi is the last page of the Old Testament, and the first thing God says on it isn’t “repent” or “I’m angry.” It’s four words: “I have loved you.” His own people look Him in the face and answer, “How?” These aren’t people who walked away. They’re church people. They show up, they bring an offering, and somewhere along the way they quit bringing their hearts.

Here’s the week. Five days in one chapter, and all of it happens around a table. God sets it with love before He says a single hard word. His people walk up carrying leftovers. God names it plainly, like a Father who won’t hang up the phone, and then He lifts your eyes past the table to the horizon, where His name is great among the nations and a Lamb has already been laid on the altar.

When you stop believing God loves you, your worship turns into leftovers. This week is about getting the love back first, because everything else grows out of it.

01

Before One Word of Correction, Love

The oracle of the word of the LORD to Israel by Malachi. “I have loved you,” says the LORD. But you say, “How have you loved us?”

Malachi 1:1 to 2 · ESV

Even the prophet’s name is a sermon. Mal’akhi means “my messenger.” He’s writing somewhere around four hundred fifty years before Christ, to a people who came home from exile and rebuilt the temple, and it didn’t look anything like they’d hoped. No cloud of glory. No king on the throne. A Persian governor, a heavy tax and a thin harvest. Disappointment sat long enough that it hardened into cynicism.

Now look at what God says first. Not a warning. Not a list. “I have loved you.” Every hard word in this book comes after that sentence, and every hard word comes from inside it. Before God corrects His people, He tells them where they stand with Him.

Their answer is a shrug with its arms crossed: “How have you loved us?” That isn’t a seeker’s question. It’s a challenge. Six times in this little book God makes a claim and His people talk back, and every argument that follows grows out of this first one. They’d quit believing He loved them, and once that goes, everything else turns into routine.

Here’s the grace hidden in the way the book is built. God doesn’t hang up. He answers. A judge reads a sentence and leaves the room; a Father stays on the line and makes His case. If that same question is sitting under your ribs today, God isn’t ashamed of you for asking it. He’s about to answer it, and His final answer is a cross. Romans roars it: “God shows his love for us in that while we were still sinners, Christ died for us.”

The Hebrew is ’ahav (אָהַב): to love. The verb in “I have loved you” is אָהַבְתִּי, ’ahavti, from ’ahav, and it’s in the perfect form. Here it speaks of love as settled fact. Not “I’m starting to love you.” Not “I’ll love you once you get it together.” I have loved you, and it’s still standing. It’s love that chose before it was ever chosen.

Before one word of correction, love. Every hard thing God says comes from inside that sentence.

Reflect

Where have you quietly asked God, “How have You loved me?”

What changes when you hear God’s love as a settled fact instead of a reward you’re still earning?

When you open your Bible, do you expect to hear correction first or love first?

02

Read His Love Off His Covenant, Not Your Crops

“Is not Esau Jacob’s brother?” declares the LORD. “Yet I have loved Jacob but Esau I have hated. I have laid waste his hill country and left his heritage to jackals of the desert.” If Edom says, “We are shattered but we will rebuild the ruins,” the LORD of hosts says, “They may build, but I will tear down, and they will be called ‘the wicked country,’ and ‘the people with whom the LORD is angry forever.’” Your own eyes shall see this, and you shall say, “Great is the LORD beyond the border of Israel!”

Malachi 1:2 to 5 · ESV

God doesn’t argue feelings. He points across the border. Jacob and Esau were twins, and the nations that came from them went two different ways. Edom said, “We’re shattered, but we’ll rebuild.” God said they’d build and He’d tear down. By Malachi’s day Babylon had already broken Edom as a nation, and other peoples had moved into its hill country. Judah got rebuilt. Edom stayed rubble. The difference wasn’t effort. The difference was God.

Hear this line the way the Bible means it, because a lot of people tense up right here. “Esau I have hated” is covenant language, and in covenant language it means not chosen for the covenant line. Genesis uses the same idiom when it says “the LORD saw that Leah was hated”; Jacob didn’t loathe Leah, he loved Rachel more. Jesus uses it when He says that anyone who “does not hate his own father and mother” can’t be His disciple, and He wasn’t telling you to despise your mama. God isn’t saying He loathed a person. He’s saying He chose a people, and He chose them by grace. Romans 9 picks up this very verse to make exactly that point: “not because of works but because of him who calls.”

Catch what Judah was doing, because we do it too. They were reading God’s love off their circumstances. Thin harvest, so God must not love us. God says no. You read My love off My covenant, not off your crops. The minute your situation gets a vote on whether God loves you, every hard season turns into a courtroom with God on trial.

Maybe the scan came back wrong. Maybe the marriage has gone cold or your kid won’t call you back. God’s final answer to “How?” isn’t Edom. It’s Calvary. First John slams the door on the doubt: “In this is love, not that we have loved God but that he loved us and sent his Son to be the propitiation for our sins.” The One who died for you was raised for you, and an empty tomb doesn’t lie about how you’re loved. Before this week’s out you’ll be saying it with Malachi: “Great is the LORD beyond the border of Israel!”

The Hebrew is sane’ (שָׂנֵא): to hate; in covenant speech, to pass over, to love less. The word behind “I have hated” is שָׂנֵאתִי, sane’ti, from sane’. It’s the same root Genesis 29:31 uses for Leah, who wasn’t loathed by Jacob but loved less than Rachel. In the language of family and covenant, the word marks who’s chosen for the line and who isn’t. It isn’t a window into God despising a child. It’s a window into grace that chooses.

You don’t measure God’s love by your last bad week. You measure it by a cross He already carried.

Reflect

What circumstance have you let cast a vote on whether God loves you?

How does it settle you to know you were chosen by grace and not because of your works?

Where can you already see God’s faithfulness reaching “beyond the border” of what you expected?

03

You Can’t Call Him Heavy and Treat Him Light

“A son honors his father, and a servant his master. If then I am a father, where is my honor? And if I am a master, where is my fear? says the LORD of hosts to you, O priests, who despise my name. But you say, ‘How have we despised your name?’ By offering polluted food upon my altar. But you say, ‘How have we polluted you?’ By saying that the LORD’s table may be despised. When you offer blind animals in sacrifice, is that not evil? And when you offer those that are lame or sick, is that not evil? Present that to your governor; will he accept you or show you favor? says the LORD of hosts.

Malachi 1:6 to 8 · ESV

Watch the order again. God calls Himself Father before He ever confronts. Identity first. You’re a son. You’re a daughter. Only then the question: “If then I am a father, where is my honor?” This is a Father’s rebuke, not a stranger’s complaint, and He only asks it because He’s already claimed you.

The priests are stunned. “How have we despised your name?” Nobody in that temple stood up and cursed God out. They just stopped weighing Him. God names it plainly: blind animals, lame animals, sick animals, the ones the market wouldn’t take, carried up to His altar as if they were good enough.

God gets practical the way a good Father does. “Present that to your governor.” Take that sick goat to the Persian official on tax day and see if he smiles. You’d never hand your boss or your banker what you’ve been handing God!

Here’s the table. Malachi calls it the LORD’s table, and God set it. He cooked the meal before you ever sat down; that’s verses 1 to 5, that’s love. His people walk up carrying the plate from the back of the fridge, the one you’d never serve company. It isn’t only goats. It’s the first hour of your day while God gets the last ten minutes before you fall asleep. It’s your best energy while He gets whatever’s left. It’s Sunday, with half of you in the room and the other half on your phone. Same system. Different plate.

Don’t hear this as a call to try harder. Hear it as a Father reminding you who you are. Sons and daughters don’t bring leftovers to a Father who’s already given them everything. They bring Him their first because they’re loved, never in order to get loved.

The Hebrew is kavod (כָּבוֹד) and bazah (בָּזָה): honor, weight; to despise, to hold cheap. When God asks “where is my honor?” He says כְבוֹדִי, kevodi, from kavod, a word built on the root for heavy. To honor someone is to let them carry weight in your life. The priests are called בּוֹזֵי שְׁמִי, bozei shemi, “despisers of my name,” from bazah: to hold cheap, to treat as worthless. It’s the same verb Genesis 25:34 uses when Esau traded his birthright for a bowl of stew: “Thus Esau despised his birthright.” Jacob’s own priests were acting like the brother God didn’t choose.

God gave His best. Don’t hand Him your leftovers.

Reflect

What gets your first and your best right now, and what does God get?

Where have you stopped weighing God without ever saying a word against Him?

What would it look like this week to bring Him your first because you’re already loved?

04

God Would Rather Lock the Doors Than Receive Empty Worship

And now entreat the favor of God, that he may be gracious to us. With such a gift from your hand, will he show favor to any of you? says the LORD of hosts. Oh that there were one among you who would shut the doors, that you might not kindle fire on my altar in vain! I have no pleasure in you, says the LORD of hosts, and I will not accept an offering from your hand.

Malachi 1:9 to 10 · ESV

Verse 9 drips with irony. Go ahead, God says, ask Me to be gracious. “With such a gift from your hand, will he show favor to any of you?” The priests were asking for God’s favor while handing Him what they wouldn’t dare hand a governor.

Verse 10 is one of the most shocking sentences in the prophets. “Oh that there were one among you who would shut the doors, that you might not kindle fire on my altar in vain!” God would rather somebody lock the building than keep the fire going for nothing. Let that sit a minute. He isn’t impressed by a busy altar. He wants a heart that means it.

That isn’t because God’s needy or insulted. It’s because He loves His people too much to let them go through the motions. A ritual with no heart in it isn’t harmless; it slowly teaches you that God is small. God would rather interrupt that than bless it.

Don’t walk away from today with only a guilt trip. We brought the blind and the lame. God brought a Lamb. John the Baptist, the very messenger Malachi promised, pointed at Jesus and shouted, “Behold, the Lamb of God, who takes away the sin of the world!” Peter tells you what your ransom cost: “the precious blood of Christ, like that of a lamb without blemish or spot.” We brought our leftovers to His table, and He laid His very best on it. On the third day He walked out of the grave, and the fire on that altar was never kindled in vain.

The Hebrew is chinnam (חִנָּם): for nothing, in vain, without cause. The little phrase “in vain” is חִנָּם, chinnam: for nothing, for free, without cause. It’s the same word Satan throws at God about Job: “Does Job fear God for no reason?” (Job 1:9). The accusation was that nobody worships God unless they’re getting paid. Malachi’s priests kept a fire burning that meant nothing, and they made the accuser’s argument for him. Job proved him wrong, and so does every heart that worships God for who He is.

We brought the blind and the lame. God brought a Lamb.

Reflect

Where has your worship turned into a fire that’s burning for nothing?

What would it mean to worship God this week for who He is, not for what you’re hoping to get?

How does the spotless Lamb change the way you walk up to God’s table?

05

He’s Not a Small God Who Settles for Scraps

For from the rising of the sun to its setting my name will be great among the nations, and in every place incense will be offered to my name, and a pure offering. For my name will be great among the nations, says the LORD of hosts. But you profane it when you say that the Lord’s table is polluted, and its fruit, that is, its food may be despised. But you say, ‘What a weariness this is,’ and you snort at it, says the LORD of hosts. You bring what has been taken by violence or is lame or sick, and this you bring as your offering! Shall I accept that from your hand? says the LORD. Cursed be the cheat who has a male in his flock, and vows it, and yet sacrifices to the Lord what is blemished. For I am a great King, says the LORD of hosts, and my name will be feared among the nations.

Malachi 1:11 to 14 · ESV

Right in the middle of the rebuke, God lifts their eyes to the horizon. You’re treating Me small. I’m not small. “From the rising of the sun to its setting my name will be great among the nations.” Israel was treating God like a little local deity having a bad century, and God announces He’s King of the whole earth.

Look where you’re reading this. Cleburne, Texas, some twenty four centuries later, with a Hebrew prophet open in front of you. Verse 11 came true, and it’s still coming true. Revelation sings the fulfillment to the Lamb: “by your blood you ransomed people for God from every tribe and language and people and nation.”

That “pure offering” isn’t a new sacrifice you’ve got to make to get right with God. Hebrews thunders it: “For by a single offering he has perfected for all time those who are being sanctified.” Jesus offered Himself once for all, and it’s finished. What we bring now flows out of that: our lives as a living sacrifice (Romans 12:1), our praise, and our generosity, “for such sacrifices are pleasing to God” (Hebrews 13:16). We don’t bring them to earn a seat at the table. We bring them because we’ve already been given one.

Back home the priests are rolling their eyes. “What a weariness this is,” and the text says they snorted at it. That’s the sound of worship that’s lost its wonder: the sigh, the huff when the service runs long. Boredom with God is the symptom. Unbelief in His love is the disease. Verse 14 adds the man who vowed a good animal and swapped in the blemished one. Nobody made him vow! He volunteered the promise and then cheated on it.

Here’s why it matters. “For I am a great King.” Everybody in Judah knew who called himself the great king; the Persian emperor did. God steps into a province run by the emperor’s man and says, I’m the great King. You’d bring the governor your best and bring Me your scraps? He’s the King of kings, and He’s the King who didn’t spare His own Son. Romans asks it with a smile: “He who did not spare his own Son but gave him up for us all, how will he not also with him graciously give us all things?”

The Hebrew is gadol (גָּדוֹל): great. Four times in this one chapter the word great lands on God. In 1:5 it’s the verb, יִגְדַּל, yigdal: “Great is the LORD beyond the border.” In 1:11 it’s the adjective twice, גָּדוֹל שְׁמִי, gadol shemi: “my name will be great among the nations.” In 1:14 it crowns the chapter: מֶלֶךְ גָּדוֹל, melekh gadol, “a great King.” God won’t let His people shrink Him down to the size of their disappointment.

He’s not a small God who settles for scraps. He’s a great King who gave His Son so you’d have a seat at His table.

Reflect

Where have you been treating God small?

What does it free in you to know the pure offering has already been made, once for all?

What’s one way you’ll bring the great King your first this week, in your time, your praise or your generosity, as worship and not as payment?

A Prayer of Identity

Father, You loved me first. I stop asking how, because I see the cross. Thank You for the Lamb You laid on Your own table, and for the empty tomb that tells me Your love is still standing. Forgive me for the leftovers, the tired prayer, the last hour, the half of me I’ve brought on Sunday. I’m not giving to earn Your love. I’m giving because I already have it. Make Your name great in me, and great in Cleburne, from the rising of the sun to its setting. Holy Spirit, fill me with wonder again, so I never look at Your table and say, “What a weariness this is.” Today and every day, I give You my first and my best. In Jesus’ name, Amen.

Declaration

I am loved by a God who chose me before I ever chose Him.
I read His love off His covenant and His cross, not off my circumstances.
He is my Father and my great King, and He carries weight in my life.
I bring Him my first and my best, never my leftovers.
The Lamb is on the table, and I have a seat at it. I walk with victory, not to it, in Jesus’ name. Amen!

Now go, loved and chosen, with a seat at the King’s table. Bring Him your first this week, because He already gave you His best. May His name be great in your home, at your work and across this city, from the rising of the sun to its setting.

Now may our Lord Jesus Christ himself, and God our Father, who loved us and gave us eternal comfort and good hope through grace, comfort your hearts and establish them in every good work and word. (2 Thessalonians 2:16 to 17)

In the name of the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit. Amen.
