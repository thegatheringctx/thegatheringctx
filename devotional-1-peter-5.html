1 and 2 Peter: Living Hope · Week 5 Devotional

Shepherds and Humility

The way up is down.

1 Peter 5:1 to 11

Peter closes his letter the way you'd least expect from a man who once swung a sword in a garden. He doesn't call a tired church to push harder. He calls it to bow lower. In this kingdom the stairs run backwards, and grace pools in the low places.

He writes to the elders first, and he doesn't write from a throne. He writes as a fellow elder, the one who swore he'd never fall and then denied his Lord three times before sunrise. Everything he says this week about humility, he learned on his face. When he tells you to clothe yourself with humility, he's already wearing the garment.

Here's the week. Five days under the mighty hand of God: shepherds who go first, an apron you never take off, a weight you throw instead of carry, an enemy who can only imitate, and a God who mends what the fire broke and does it Himself.

01

Shepherd the Flock of God

shepherd the flock of God that is among you, exercising oversight, not under compulsion, but willingly, as God would have you; not for shameful gain, but eagerly; not domineering over those in your charge, but being examples to the flock. And when the chief Shepherd appears, you will receive the unfading crown of glory.

1 Peter 5:2 to 4 · ESV

Look at the word that opens the chapter, even though today's reading starts a verse later. So. Peter isn't opening a new topic, he's answering the one he just left you with. Chapter 4 ended: entrust your souls to a faithful Creator while doing good. Entrust is a banking word, a deposit handed over for safekeeping. So I exhort the elders. This whole chapter, starting right here with shepherds, is Peter showing you what entrusting your soul to God actually looks like with skin on it.

Peter's talking to elders first, but don't skip today because nobody ever handed you a title. You lead somebody. A home, a team, a crew at work, a small group, a younger believer who watches how you handle a hard week. Peter's charge reaches all of it.

Notice whose flock it is. Not the elder's flock; the flock of God. The people you lead are on loan. They belong to Him, and you'll answer to Him for how you treated them. That changes the whole posture. You don't own them, so you can't use them. You're keeping them for Someone.

Peter knows exactly where he heard this charge. On a beach after the resurrection, over a charcoal fire, the risen Jesus asked him three times whether he loved Him, once for every denial, and three times He handed the same answer back: feed my sheep. The man who failed beside one fire got restored beside another and handed a flock.

Peter names the three diseases every leader carries into the room: reluctance, greed and control. Under compulsion, for shameful gain, domineering. The cure for all three is the same. Be an example. Don't point at the road; walk it in front of them.

The Greek is poimainō (ποιμαίνω): to tend a flock; to lead, feed, guard and care for sheep. It isn't a word for driving cattle or managing staff. A shepherd in Peter's world walked in front of the flock, found the grass, fought off whatever came for the sheep and slept across the gate. It's the same verb Jesus used on the beach when He told Peter, Tend my sheep. It's a verb that costs the one doing it. If your leadership has started costing the people instead of costing you, it's drifted from poimainō into something else.

Lead for the crown that doesn't fade, and you won't sell out for the applause that does.

Reflect

Who's in your charge right now, even if nobody gave you a title for it?

Of reluctance, greed and control, which one creeps into how you lead?

What would it look like this week to walk the road in front of them instead of pointing at it?

02

Tie On the Apron

Likewise, you who are younger, be subject to the elders. Clothe yourselves, all of you, with humility toward one another, for 'God opposes the proud but gives grace to the humble.'

1 Peter 5:5 · ESV

Peter turns from the elders to everybody. All of you. Nobody in the church is exempt from this one, and nobody gets promoted past it.

He had a reason to reach for the picture he chose. The night before the cross, Jesus got up from supper, laid aside His outer garments, tied a towel around His waist and washed His disciples' feet. Peter was the one who pulled his feet back: You shall never wash my feet. He watched God on His knees with a towel. Decades later, when he wants to show a church what humility looks like, that's what he sees.

Here's why it matters so much: God opposes the proud but gives grace to the humble. Pride doesn't just make you hard to be around. It puts you in opposition to God Himself, pushing against the very hand that wants to bless you.

Grace runs downhill. It pools in the low places. If you want more of it, you don't climb toward it; you get lower, and you let Him do the lifting at the proper time.

The Greek is enkomboomai (ἐγκομβόομαι): to tie on, to fasten on with a knot; from the family of words for the apron a servant tied over his clothes. It appears only here in the whole New Testament. Peter could've used the everyday word for getting dressed; he chose the knot. An apron you tie on is meant to stay on through the work, and it tells everybody in the room what you came to do. Humility isn't a mood you drift into on a quiet morning. It's something you tie on on purpose, toward one another, and keep tied.

Grace runs downhill. If you want more of it, get lower.

Reflect

Where have you been waiting to feel humble instead of choosing to tie it on?

Who would you find it hardest to serve this week, and why?

Where might pride be pushing against the very hand that wants to bless you?

03

Throw It on the One Who Cares

Humble yourselves, therefore, under the mighty hand of God so that at the proper time he may exalt you, casting all your anxieties on him, because he cares for you.

1 Peter 5:6 to 7 · ESV

Right in the middle of the call to humility, Peter drops the tenderest line in the letter. Don't read it as a change of subject. It's the same subject. It's even the same command he gave back in 4:19, entrust your souls to a faithful Creator, just wearing a different word. There, entrust. Here, cast. One is a deposit for safekeeping; the other is a decisive throw. Same move, said twice.

Anxiety is often pride in disguise: the belief that it's all on you and you have to carry it. Humbling yourself under God's mighty hand means admitting you can't carry it, and letting Someone who can take it off your back.

Hear this carefully, because it matters. If you're fighting anxiety with counseling or with medicine, that isn't pride, and this isn't a word against getting help. Peter's aiming at the weight you keep insisting on carrying alone.

Why can you let it go? Because He cares for you. The mighty hand you're under isn't cold. It's the hand of a Father who's thinking about you right now. David knew it a thousand years before Peter wrote it down: Cast your burden on the LORD, and he will sustain you.

The Greek is epiriptō (ἐπιρίπτω): to throw upon; to hurl something onto someone else. It isn't a gentle setting down. It's a decisive throw, the way you'd fling a heavy load off your own back onto a cart. The word shows up only one other time in the New Testament, in Luke 19:35, where the disciples threw their cloaks onto the colt and Jesus rode into Jerusalem on what they'd thrown down. The only other time the word appears, what got thrown down carried the King. What you throw onto Him ends up under the One who carries it, and He carries it better than you ever did.

Name the thing that's been crushing you, and throw it.

Reflect

What exactly are you carrying today that you were never meant to carry alone?

Where has "it's all on me" quietly become a kind of pride?

What would it look like to throw it today, out loud, instead of setting it down politely and picking it back up tomorrow?

04

The Lion Is an Impersonator

Be sober-minded; be watchful. Your adversary the devil prowls around like a roaring lion, seeking someone to devour. Resist him, firm in your faith, knowing that the same kinds of suffering are being experienced by your brotherhood throughout the world.

1 Peter 5:8 to 9 · ESV

Peter has one more warning before he blesses them. You have an enemy, and he isn't imaginary. The devil prowls around like a roaring lion, looking for someone to devour.

Read it close: like a roaring lion. He's an impersonator. Your adversary can only imitate what Jesus actually is, because Revelation says the Lion of the tribe of Judah has conquered. You're not hiding from a lion today. You're standing behind One.

Notice who lions hunt. Not the herd; the straggler, the one that's tired and wandered off alone. That's why Peter has spent the whole chapter on humility and on staying together. The sheep that stays low and stays close isn't easy prey. Isolation feels like rest when you're worn out, but it's usually just exposure.

Resist him, Peter says, firm in your faith. Not by cleverness and not by fear; by standing on what you know about God. He adds a strange comfort, too. Your brothers and sisters around the world are going through the same kinds of suffering. You're not being singled out. You're walking a road a whole family walks.

The Greek is nēphō (νήφω): to be sober; to keep a clear head, free from whatever dulls your judgment. Peter uses it three times in this short letter, in 1:13, 4:7 and here in 5:8, and every time it sits right beside something he wants you awake for: your hope, your prayers, and now the enemy. It's the opposite of fog. Worry fogs you, exhaustion fogs you, isolation fogs you. Peter's call isn't to be afraid of the lion. It's to be awake enough to see him coming and close enough to the flock that he can't cut you out.

You're not hiding from a lion. You're standing behind One.

Reflect

Where have you been drifting off alone because you're tired?

What fog, whether worry, exhaustion or distraction, is dulling how clearly you see right now?

Who could you reach toward this week so that neither of you walks alone?

05

The God Who Mends

And after you have suffered a little while, the God of all grace, who has called you to his eternal glory in Christ, will himself restore, confirm, strengthen, and establish you. To him be the dominion forever and ever. Amen.

1 Peter 5:10 to 11 · ESV

Peter ends the letter the only way this letter could end. Not on the lion; on a promise.

Stack the four verbs. Restore what the fire damaged. Confirm you, so you aren't shaken. Strengthen you where you're weak. Establish you on a foundation that can't move.

Look at the two small words that carry the whole thing: he himself. God doesn't send the repair from a distance. He comes down and does it with His own hands. Your suffering is a little while; His restoration is forever.

Peter knows this from the inside. On the night of the Last Supper, Jesus told him Satan had demanded to sift him like wheat, and then said, I have prayed for you that your faith may not fail. And when you have turned again, strengthen your brothers. The lion did come for Peter, and Peter did fall. Jesus went to the cross anyway, walked out of the grave anyway, found Peter anyway, restored him and put him to work. When this man writes that the God of all grace will Himself restore you, he isn't quoting a verse he likes. He's telling you what was done to him.

The Greek is katartizō (καταρτίζω): to mend, to repair, to put back in proper order. It's the word Mark uses for James and John sitting in their boat mending their nets, and it was used in the ancient medical world for setting a broken bone. Peter heard it first as a fisherman's word; he'd spent years watching torn nets get mended so they could go back into the water. That's the promise. God doesn't throw away what the fire tore. He mends it Himself and sends it back out to fish.

Your suffering is a little while. His restoration is forever.

Reflect

Where do you most need God to mend what the fire broke?

Of the four verbs, restore, confirm, strengthen and establish, which do you need most this week?

Peter was restored and handed a job. Who might God be asking you to strengthen, now that you've turned again?

A Prayer of Identity

Father, thank You that the way up in Your kingdom is down, and that Your grace pools in the low places. Thank You for the Chief Shepherd, who laid down His life for the sheep and took it up again. Teach me to lead like Him, going first and not pushing from behind, remembering that the people in my charge are Yours. I tie humility on today, the way Jesus tied on a towel. I throw my anxieties onto You, every one of them, because You care for me. Keep me sober and watchful, close to Your people and not wandering off alone. Where the fire has cracked me, mend me Yourself. Restore, confirm, strengthen and establish me, and finish what You started. In Jesus' name, Amen.

Declaration

I clothe myself with humility, because God gives grace to the humble.
I humble myself under the mighty hand of God, and He will lift me at the proper time.
I cast all my anxiety on Him, because He cares for me.
I stand firm against the enemy, sober and watchful, not wandering off alone.
The God of all grace will Himself restore, confirm, strengthen, and establish me.
I walk with victory, not to it, in Jesus' name. Amen.

Now go, and take the lower place, where the grace is. May those who lead do it as shepherds and examples, under the Chief Shepherd. May you clothe yourself with humility and throw every anxiety onto the God who cares. May you stand firm against the enemy, and may the God of all grace Himself restore, confirm, strengthen, and establish you.

Now to him who is able to keep you from stumbling and to present you blameless before the presence of his glory with great joy, to the only God, our Savior, through Jesus Christ our Lord, be glory, majesty, dominion, and authority, before all time and now and forever. Amen. (Jude 24 to 25)
