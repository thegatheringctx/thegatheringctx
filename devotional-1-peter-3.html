1 and 2 Peter: Living Hope · Week 3 Devotional

Through the Water

He made the vow first, and He kept it alone.

1 Peter 3:1 to 22

This is the chapter nobody wants to open, because this is where Peter says the quiet part out loud. You can do the right thing and still get hurt for it, and God calls that blessed.

Watch where Peter starts. He begins not at the courthouse and not at the coliseum, but at the kitchen table, with a wife married to a man who wants nothing to do with Jesus. From there the same faithfulness walks out onto the street, where blessing your enemy costs you something real. Finally, when you are certain you cannot carry it, he turns and shows you the vow that was kept first: Christ suffered once, the righteous for the unrighteous, to bring you to God.

That is where the week lands, and it lands in water. Peter says baptism is an appeal to God for a good conscience. It's a yes, said out loud, in front of witnesses, with your whole body. This Sunday some of us are going to say it in the tank. For five days, we look at the One who said it first.

01

The Hidden Person of the Heart

Do not let your adorning be external, the braiding of hair and the putting on of gold jewelry, or the clothing you wear, but let your adorning be the hidden person of the heart with the imperishable beauty of a gentle and quiet spirit, which in God's sight is very precious.

1 Peter 3:3 to 4 · ESV

Peter isn't banning jewelry; he's moving the mirror. Everything on the outside is perishable, and he already used that word back in chapter 1 for the inheritance that can't rot. Here he says there's a beauty in you that belongs in that same category. It doesn't fade, and God calls it very precious.

Now hear where he puts it: the hidden person of the heart. Not the version of you that shows up in a photograph, and not the version that gets the compliment, but the one underneath, the one nobody applauds and God treasures.

The two words he chooses, gentle and quiet, are the ones we've been taught to read as weakness. That isn't what they mean, and it isn't what Peter means, because six verses later he binds a husband with the strongest language in the chapter. Gentleness in this letter is never the absence of strength; it's strength that already decided who it answers to.

The Greek is praus (πραΰς), gentle, meek, strength that is under control. It's the word used for a warhorse broken to the bridle: every ounce of the power is still in the animal, it just answers to somebody now. Get it out of your head that gentle means you have nothing to fight with. The gentle person has plenty and has handed the reins to God. That's why this beauty is imperishable. Looks fade because they were never the strength; this one is your strength, yielded, and it'll still be there when the mirror stops cooperating.

A gentle spirit isn't weakness; it's your strength, yielded to God, and it'll preach where your words never could.

Reflect

Where have you been tending the outside and letting the hidden person of the heart go untended?

Have you been reading gentleness as having nothing to fight with, when Peter reads it as strength with the reins handed over?

What would it change today to believe God calls that hidden beauty very precious?

02

On the Contrary, Bless

Finally, all of you, have unity of mind, sympathy, brotherly love, a tender heart, and a humble mind. Do not repay evil for evil or reviling for reviling, but on the contrary, bless, for to this you were called, that you may obtain a blessing.

1 Peter 3:8 to 9 · ESV

Three words carry this whole verse: on the contrary. Your flesh already drafted the reply at two in the morning, insult for insult. They went low, you go lower, and you call it standing up for yourself. Peter says go the opposite direction entirely and bless them.

Don't miss what's hanging off the end of it: to this you were called, that you may obtain a blessing. Blessing your enemy isn't the losing move it feels like. You don't get emptied by it; you inherit, because in that moment you acted like your Father, who blessed every one of us while we were still His enemies.

Notice the order, because this is the whole letter in miniature. Peter doesn't say bless people so that God will finally start blessing you. He says you were called into a blessing you already have, and blessing them is what people who own that inheritance do. You aren't earning the family name; you're carrying it.

Here's the honest part: this one doesn't get easy. There are names you can put in this sentence today where blessing them is still not automatic on a Wednesday. Peter isn't handing you a scorecard; he's about to hand you the only fuel that makes it possible.

You don't lose by blessing your enemy; you inherit, because you acted like your Father.

Reflect

Whose name goes in this sentence for you, and what would blessing them actually look like this week?

Where have you been calling retaliation self respect?

Have you been trying to bless in order to get a blessing, instead of blessing out of one you already have?

03

Who Sits on the Throne

But even if you should suffer for righteousness' sake, you will be blessed. Have no fear of them, nor be troubled, but in your hearts honor Christ the Lord as holy, always being prepared to make a defense to anyone who asks you for a reason for the hope that is in you; yet do it with gentleness and respect.

1 Peter 3:14 to 15 · ESV

Look at the swap Peter makes. He doesn't say try harder not to be afraid. He says put Somebody else on the throne. When your heart is packed full of what people think of you, there's no room left in it to honor Christ. You can't bow to Jesus and tremble in front of your critics in the same heart. One of them gets the throne today.

This line is bigger than it looks. Peter is quoting Isaiah 8, where God told a terrified nation not to fear what everybody else feared, but to honor the LORD of hosts as holy. That's Yahweh in Isaiah's sentence. Peter picks the verse up and drops one name into the slot: honor Christ the Lord as holy. The fisherman who ate breakfast on a beach with Jesus is telling you the man he shared fish with is the Holy One of Isaiah.

Out of a heart like that comes a witness. Notice Peter assumes people will ask. He expects your life to be running so hopeful and so unbothered that somebody eventually walks up and wants to know what's wrong with you.

The word for that answer is apologia (ἀπολογία), a defense, a reasoned answer, the case you would make in front of a judge. It's courtroom language, and it's where we get the word apologetics. Look at what Peter asks you to defend, though: not a system, not a denomination, not an argument, but the hope that is in you. He isn't asking you to win a debate. He's asking you to be able to say, in your own words, to the actual person in front of you, why you aren't falling apart. He sets the tone in the same breath: gentleness and fear. You can be right and wrong at the same time. Win the argument, lose the person, and you didn't win anything.

Count the fears in this chapter. Verse 6, do not fear anything that is frightening. Verse 14, have no fear of them. Verse 15, with gentleness and fear, which your ESV translates respect. Peter never told you to stop fearing; he told you to move it. Take it off the people who are talking about you and set it back on the God who is not.

Reflect

Whose opinion has been sitting on the throne where Christ belongs?

If somebody asked you today for a reason for your hope, what would you actually say, and could you say it gently?

Where do you need to move your fear rather than try to kill it?

04

The Righteous for the Unrighteous

For Christ also suffered once for sins, the righteous for the unrighteous, that he might bring us to God, being put to death in the flesh but made alive in the spirit.

1 Peter 3:18 · ESV

Here's the ground under the whole chapter. Why bless the one who's wounding you? Why keep doing good when it's costing you? Because Christ suffered first, and that's the only reason any of us are here at all.

Look at the exchange: the righteous for the unrighteous. He had nothing of His own to answer for. He answered for yours, and He did it on purpose.

Now weigh that word once, because the old system leaned the other way entirely. Year after year the priest walked back in carrying more blood, because it was never finished. Hebrews says it plainly, those sacrifices could never take away sins, which is exactly why they had to keep repeating. Peter says once. One offering, never again. The endless line of lambs finally stopped at a cross, because the last Lamb was enough.

Now read where the verse is going, because it's the most beautiful phrase in the letter: that he might bring us to God. That's what the cross was for, not to forgive you from a safe distance and leave you standing outside the door, but to bring you all the way in.

The word is prosago (προσάγω), to bring to, to lead in, to usher someone into the presence of a king. It's access language. In the Old Testament it's the word for a priest being presented before the LORD. Christ didn't suffer to improve you; He suffered to present you. You aren't barely tolerated at the edge of God's presence, hoping nobody looks too closely. You were escorted in by the Son, and He is not embarrassed to be seen with you.

Christ didn't suffer to improve you; He suffered to present you.

Reflect

Do you live like someone forgiven from a distance, or like someone escorted in?

What are you still trying to offer God that His one offering already covered?

How does the word once change the way you carry yesterday's failure today?

05

An Appeal to God

Baptism, which corresponds to this, now saves you, not as a removal of dirt from the body but as an appeal to God for a good conscience, through the resurrection of Jesus Christ, who has gone into heaven and is at the right hand of God, with angels, authorities, and powers having been subjected to him.

1 Peter 3:21 to 22 · ESV

Peter has just reached back for Noah, and it's worth standing still in it. The same water that judged the world is the water that lifted the ark. Judgment on the outside, salvation on the inside. The ark took the storm so the family inside it didn't have to. That's a cross made out of wood. Christ took the flood, and everybody hidden in Him rides it all the way up.

Then comes the sentence that has confused people for two thousand years: baptism now saves you. Read the whole verse, because Peter answers the question himself in the same breath. Not as a removal of dirt from the body. The water doesn't wash sin. If you go down a sinner, you come up a wet sinner.

What it is, is an appeal, an answer, a pledge. It's your conscience responding to God, and the power in it belongs entirely to the resurrection of Jesus Christ. The water saves the way a ring marries. Nobody is married by jewelry, yet you don't keep it in a drawer either, because it's the sign and the pledge of a covenant already cut. Grace did the saving, faith received it, and the water announces it out loud in front of everybody.

The word Peter uses is eperotema (ἐπερώτημα), an appeal, an answer given to a question, a pledge made in response. It carries the sense of a formal answer, the kind you give when you're asked something in front of witnesses and cannot answer it halfway. That's what baptism is. The question got asked at a cross, and baptism is your conscience answering it out loud, in front of the room, with your whole body. Verse 22 tells you why the answer holds: the One who suffered is now at the right hand of God, with every power subjected to Him. The One who suffered now reigns.

Here's what you carry out of this week. You aren't the first one in this covenant to say yes. He said it first, in a garden, with His face in the dirt and sweat falling like drops of blood: not my will but Yours. He said it knowing exactly what it would cost, which is more than any of us knew at our own altar. He made the vow, He kept the vow, and He kept it alone so that you would never have to keep it alone.

The water doesn't wash the body; it announces the conscience. Baptism is a good conscience going public with its resurrection.

Reflect

If you've believed and never been baptized, what has been holding your yes back from going public?

Where have you been treating the sign as if it were the salvation, or treating the salvation as if it needed no sign?

How does it steady you to know Christ made the vow first, kept it perfectly, and kept it alone?

A Prayer of Identity

Father, thank You that the beauty You treasure in me is the hidden person of the heart, and that it doesn't fade. Make my gentleness the kind that has strength in it and has handed You the reins. When I'm reviled, keep me from repaying it, and let me bless instead, because You blessed me while I was still Your enemy. Take the throne of my heart, so the fear of people shrinks down in front of You, and give me a ready answer for the hope that's in me. Thank You that Christ suffered once, the righteous for the unrighteous, not to improve me but to present me, all the way into Your presence. Thank You too that the water announces what grace already did. He said yes first and kept it alone, so today I live like someone who's already been brought home. In Jesus' name, Amen.

Declaration

The beauty God treasures in me is imperishable, and it lives in the hidden person of my heart.

I don't repay evil for evil. I bless, because I've been blessed.

Christ sits on the throne of my heart, so the fear of people has no room left.

I'm always ready to give a reason for the hope that is in me, with gentleness and respect.

Christ suffered once, the righteous for the unrighteous, and He brought me to God.

I walk with victory, not to it, in Jesus' name. Amen.

Now go, and let the hidden person of your heart be adorned with a beauty that cannot fade. May you never repay evil for evil, but bless, for to this you were called, that you may obtain a blessing. May you honor Christ the Lord as holy in your heart, always ready to give a reason for the hope that is in you, with gentleness and respect. May the water preach to you all week, because Christ suffered once, the righteous for the unrighteous, to bring you to God, and He is not asking for it back.

Now may the God of peace who brought again from the dead our Lord Jesus, the great shepherd of the sheep, by the blood of the eternal covenant, equip you with everything good that you may do his will, working in us that which is pleasing in his sight, through Jesus Christ, to whom be glory forever and ever. Amen. (Hebrews 13:20 to 21)

In the name of the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit. Amen.
